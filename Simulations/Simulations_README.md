# Brownian diffusion simulations.

Simulated image stacks were generated by modeling fluorescent particles undergoing two-dimensional Brownian diffusion. Particle positions were initialized randomly within the field of view and updated at each frame by random displacements drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 2DΔt. Each particle was rendered as a Gaussian spot with a width sampled from a normal distribution around the specified mean value, thereby accounting for particle-to-particle variability. Individual particle intensities were summed to generate the synthetic fluorescence image, Gaussian background noise was added, and an optional exponential photobleaching term was applied to the particle intensity throughout the acquisition. The resulting image sequences were saved as TIFF stacks and analyzed using the same iMSD workflow applied to experimental datasets. Unless otherwise specified, simulated image stacks consisted of 2,000 frames of 128 × 128 pixels, with a pixel size of 0.05 µm and a frame interval of 0.01 s.

# Confined diffusion simulations.

Confined diffusion was simulated by modeling particle trajectories as two-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes. Each particle diffused around a randomly assigned confinement center under the action of a linear restoring force, producing stationary trajectories characterized by a confinement size and a characteristic confinement time. At each frame, particles were rendered as Gaussian intensity distributions with widths randomly sampled from a normal distribution. Individual particle contributions were summed to generate the simulated fluorescence image, after which additive Gaussian background noise and first-order photobleaching were applied. Unless otherwise specified, simulated image stacks consisted of 2,000 frames of 128 × 128 pixels, with a pixel size of 0.05 μm and a frame interval of 0.01 s.

# Transient confinement simulations.

Transient confinement was simulated by modeling two-dimensional Brownian diffusion within a regular lattice of square compartments separated by semipermeable barriers. At each time step, particle displacements were sampled from a Gaussian distribution corresponding to the prescribed diffusion coefficient. Whenever a particle attempted to cross a compartment boundary, the crossing event was accepted with probability P. Otherwise, the displacement component perpendicular to the barrier was rejected while the tangential component was preserved. This procedure generated trajectories exhibiting transient confinement, with free Brownian diffusion recovered for P=1 and permanent confinement obtained for P=0. Particles were rendered as Gaussian intensity distributions with widths randomly sampled from a normal distribution, and synthetic fluorescence images were generated by summing the contribution of all particles. Additive Gaussian background noise and first-order photobleaching were subsequently applied. Unless otherwise specified, simulated image stacks consisted of 2,000 frames of 128 × 128 pixels, with a pixel size of 0.05 μm and a frame interval of 0.01 s.

# Active transport simulations.

Synthetic image stacks describing directed intracellular transport were generated by simulating fluorescent particles moving within a random network of linear guiding paths. A set of randomly positioned and randomly oriented linear tracks was generated across the simulated field of view. At each simulation step, the distance of every particle from the nearest track was evaluated. Particles located within the track width underwent Brownian diffusion with diffusion coefficient Din, superimposed to a constant drift velocity v directed along the local track orientation, thereby reproducing active transport. Particles outside the guided regions diffused isotropically with diffusion coefficient Dout. Particle trajectories were reflected at the image boundaries to prevent escape from the simulated field of view. Fluorescence images were generated by rendering each particle as a two-dimensional Gaussian intensity profile with a normally distributed width, followed by the addition of Gaussian detector noise and exponential photobleaching. Unless otherwise specified, simulated image stacks consisted of 2,000 frames of 128 × 128 pixels, with a pixel size of 0.05 μm and a frame interval of 1 ms.

# Dynamic partitioning simulations.

Dynamic partitioning was simulated by modeling fluorescent particles that alternate between a freely diffusing state and a transiently trapped state within fixed nanodomains. A set of randomly positioned circular domains, each much smaller than the diffraction-limited spot, was distributed across the field of view. At each time step, particle displacements were sampled from a Gaussian distribution corresponding to the extradomain diffusion coefficient D_out outside the domains and to the intradomain coefficient D_in inside them. A freely diffusing particle whose step brought it inside a domain was trapped with probability P_in, whereas a trapped particle escaped by crossing the domain boundary with probability P_out; this yielded an exponentially distributed trapping (residence) time τT that scales inversely with P_out (τT ≈ Δt/P_out). The domains were provided in large excess over the particles, so that trapping events remained independent and the domains largely unsaturated, ensuring that the trapped population decorrelated as a single exponential. Particles were rendered as Gaussian intensity distributions with widths randomly sampled from a normal distribution, and synthetic fluorescence images were generated by summing the contribution of all particles, followed by the addition of Gaussian background noise and first-order photobleaching. Unless otherwise specified, simulated image stacks consisted of 2,000 frames of 128 × 128 pixels, with a pixel size of 0.05 μm and a frame interval of 2 ms.
